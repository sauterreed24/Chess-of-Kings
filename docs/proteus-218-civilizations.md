# Proteus 218 Civilization Bible

This document deepens the playable civilizations for **Proteus 218: River of Crowns**, the land-only drought-war strategy concept. It is written as implementation guidance for Codex and as a creative reference for future story, duel mode, balance, AI, UI, and campaign work.

## Core civilization design law

Every civilization must be strong because of its home, history, and survival logic. No faction is evil. No faction is a reskin. No faction should be balanced by blandness.

Each civilization needs:

1. A believable homeland shaped by the Aurelian River and drought pressure.
2. A civic myth that explains why its people think their war is necessary.
3. A battlefield doctrine that creates a different rhythm of play.
4. An economy that creates distinct short, medium, and long-term decisions.
5. A weakness that smart opponents can exploit without feeling cheap.
6. A campaign arc that makes the player respect them even when fighting them.
7. A duel-mode identity that can be understood quickly but mastered slowly.

The player should not ask, “Which civilization has the best stats?” The player should ask, “Which theory of survival do I believe in this match?”

## Shared world concepts

### The Aurelian River

The Aurelian is not just a river. It is the civilizational spine of Proteus 218. It provides drinking water, ritual authority, farming cycles, roads, flood calendars, legal boundaries, and military logistics. When the drought begins, every civilization learns that control of water is also control of time.

### The River Concord

For millennia, the Concord regulated water rights, bridge tolls, floodplain harvests, military obligations, and engineering knowledge. Its collapse creates the tragedy of the commons: every faction acts rationally for itself, but those actions accelerate planetary catastrophe.

### The War of Seven Gates

Seven ancient hydraulic gates once balanced water between regions. During the drought, each gate becomes a fortress, shrine, factory, and hostage all at once. Campaign chapters should use these gates as recurring objectives.

### Total victory without cartoon evil

Total victory means breaking an enemy’s power to continue the war: citadels captured, armies routed, roads cut, gates seized, or command legitimacy destroyed. The defeated faction should not be treated as exterminated. The game can show surrender, annexation, hostage courts, oath-taking, exile, relief convoys, and reluctant integration without gore.

## Universal mechanics that make the civilizations unique

### 1. Water tempo

Water is not just food. It is tempo. Armies far from wells lose readiness. Cities without reservoir access cannot sustain long recruitment. River crossings become timing puzzles.

### 2. Doctrine stress

Every faction has a doctrine meter. It rewards playing according to that civilization’s worldview, but if pushed too far it creates civil strain. Example: the Valeric Road League gains power from connected roads, but if it overextends roads into hostile territory, corruption and ambush risk rise.

### 3. Oath burden

Captured cities can be annexed, protected, stripped, or militarized. Each choice changes economy, legitimacy, rebellion risk, and campaign dialogue. This keeps conquest central while making conquest morally and strategically weighty.

### 4. Command geometry

Armies perform best when formations follow their doctrine shape: Valerics in layered road-supported lines, Sareth in crescent raids, Orosians in anchored blocks, Veyrans around engineered kill zones. This gives each faction a chess-like geometry.

### 5. Drought adaptation trees

Each civilization has three progression branches:

- **Concord Legacy:** preserve old institutions, stronger stability and civic bonuses.
- **Emergency Power:** militarize survival systems, stronger war output with civil costs.
- **Drought Innovation:** adapt terrain, logistics, and doctrine, strongest late-game flexibility.

## Civilization 1: Valeric Road League

### Identity

The Valerics are the inheritors of the old road law. Their cities are arranged like beads on paved causeways, fortified mile-stations, aqueduct spurs, and legion schools. They believe peace existed because someone maintained the roads, counted the grain, enforced bridge law, and punished bandit kings.

Their tragedy is that they confuse order with legitimacy. When the drought begins, they are the first to mobilize “temporary emergency legions” to protect transport corridors. Other civilizations see this as occupation.

### Homeland

Central river belt with rolling farmland, old roads, stone bridges, and midstream administrative capitals. Their maps have many roads and forts but fewer hidden resources.

### Strategic fantasy

The Valeric player feels like a Roman crisis commander: disciplined, logistical, layered, hard to crack, and excellent at turning small advantages into unstoppable campaigns.

### Core mechanic: Road Mandate

Connected roads generate command, supply speed, reinforcement speed, and tax stability. A Valeric army fighting within road influence gains formation recovery and faster rally after morale shocks.

Overextension risk: roads outside secure territory become liability points. Enemy raiders can create road panic, tax loss, and delayed reinforcements.

### Economy

Strong at medium-term scaling. Their economy is not the fastest at raw extraction, but it is the most reliable when roads are intact. City governors gain extra efficiency when connected to two or more allied settlements.

### Army doctrine

Layered combined arms. Shields hold. Spears protect flanks. Archers soften. Cavalry pursues. Engineers build roads and field camps.

### Unique units

- **Milestone Cohort:** heavy shield infantry that gains cohesion near roads, forts, and command posts.
- **Surveyor Engineers:** build roads, watch posts, temporary bridges, and siege ramps faster than normal engineers.
- **Censor Guard:** command infantry that improves nearby morale but increases civil strain if used to suppress captured cities.
- **Causeway Cavalry:** medium cavalry with faster reinforcement movement along roads.

### Unique structures

- **Mile Fort:** small road fort that extends supply and vision.
- **Civic Granary:** stabilizes cities during drought, less efficient if isolated.
- **Tribunal Gate:** reduces unrest in annexed cities but slows local production while legal integration happens.

### Progression branches

- **Concord Legacy:** stronger annexation, lower rebellion, better city stability.
- **Emergency Power:** cheaper cohorts, faster forts, more command pressure, higher civil strain.
- **Drought Innovation:** water-cart logistics, road cisterns, better resilience when disconnected.

### Weaknesses

- Predictable expansion paths.
- Vulnerable to raids that cut roads.
- Slower early aggression than cavalry or ambush factions.
- Civil strain rises if conquest is too militarized.

### Best counters

Sareth raiders can cut roads. Lyridian ambushers punish overbuilt corridors. Helakar attrition can force Valerics to defend too many posts.

### Campaign role

The Valerics begin as peacekeepers and become occupiers by accident. Their central question: “Can law survive when every law requires force?”

### AI personality

Methodical expansion, road repair priority, fortified pushes, disciplined retreats. The AI should feel fair, stubborn, and increasingly suffocating if ignored.

## Civilization 2: Nareth Terrace Cities

### Identity

The Nareth live downstream in terraced floodplain cities built on centuries of irrigation mathematics. Their culture revolves around calendars, harvest songs, reservoir oaths, and public granaries. They believe civilization is measured by how well it feeds the vulnerable.

Their tragedy is dependence. The drought makes them the most desperate because upstream decisions can starve them no matter how wisely they govern.

### Homeland

Lower river terraces, delta-like marshless floodplains, reservoir basins, orchard belts, and dense city networks. There are no oceans, but the lower river widens into braided freshwater basins.

### Strategic fantasy

The Nareth player feels like a brilliant defensive economist who transforms fragile water access into overwhelming late-game production.

### Core mechanic: Reservoir Covenant

Reservoirs store water across seasons. Nareth cities can bank water surplus and spend it to stabilize farms, accelerate training, resist sieges, or support distant armies.

Drought risk: if reservoirs are captured or drained, the Nareth suffer sharper collapse than other factions.

### Economy

Powerful growth and food production if protected. They can sustain larger armies and recover from crop shocks, but must defend water infrastructure.

### Army doctrine

Defensive depth, missile pressure, fortified terraces, counterattacks after enemy exhaustion.

### Unique units

- **Terrace Archers:** archers that gain range/accuracy on elevated farms, walls, and ridges.
- **Reservoir Wardens:** spear-shield infantry with bonuses near waterworks and city gates.
- **Canal Runners:** light infantry that move quickly through irrigated terrain and repair water structures.
- **Flood Levy:** emergency militia trained quickly when a reservoir city is threatened.

### Unique structures

- **Step Reservoir:** stores water and boosts food, but becomes a major enemy target.
- **Terrace Wall:** defensive wall/farm hybrid, weaker than a fortress but boosts adjacent farms.
- **Relief Hall:** reduces unrest and famine penalties in conquered or allied cities.

### Progression branches

- **Concord Legacy:** relief systems, diplomacy-flavored stability without diplomatic victory.
- **Emergency Power:** flood levy, harsher rationing, faster defensive mobilization.
- **Drought Innovation:** drought crops, deep cisterns, mobile water trains.

### Weaknesses

- Vulnerable to upstream gate control.
- Infrastructure-heavy and target-rich.
- Less mobile in open terrain.
- Early military pressure can prevent their economy from blooming.

### Best counters

Veyran gate seizure can choke them. Sareth raids can drain reservoirs. Valeric legal occupation can slowly absorb their city networks.

### Campaign role

The Nareth first plead for fairness, then seize gates when fairness fails. Their central question: “Is stealing water still theft if your children are dying?”

### AI personality

Builds defensively, protects reservoirs, counterattacks when enemy supply is low, seeks high-ground archer positions.

## Civilization 3: Orosian Highland Cantons

### Identity

The Orosians live in iron highlands, cold basins, cliff roads, and fortress towns. Their political unit is the canton: small, stubborn, oath-bound, and suspicious of river empires. They are not expansionist by temperament, but the drought turns their mountain springs into priceless strategic assets.

Their tragedy is that neutrality becomes impossible. Everyone wants their springs and iron.

### Homeland

High ridges, mountain passes, iron mines, spring-fed valleys, and defensible plateaus.

### Strategic fantasy

The Orosian player feels like a Spartan-Macedonian mountain commander: slow, disciplined, brutally hard to dislodge, and lethal when terrain is chosen well.

### Core mechanic: Anchor Points

Orosian formations gain bonuses when anchored to terrain: cliffs, ridges, walls, passes, stakes, or allied formations. Properly anchored formations resist flanking and morale shock.

Mobility risk: once committed, they reposition slowly. Bad deployment is punished.

### Economy

Excellent iron and stone. Moderate food. Water is secure in springs but limited in volume, so giant armies are hard to sustain.

### Army doctrine

Defensive spears, heavy infantry, pass control, deliberate counterpushes.

### Unique units

- **Canton Phalanx:** spear formation with huge frontal strength when anchored, weak when flanked or exhausted.
- **Iron Oathbreakers:** elite shock infantry made from disgraced warriors seeking restoration. Strong but politically sensitive.
- **Passwardens:** light mountain scouts with vision and ambush detection.
- **Spring Guard:** defensive infantry that improves water security and city morale.

### Unique structures

- **Canton Hold:** compact fortress that boosts nearby militia and stores iron.
- **Spring House:** protects water supply and reduces drought impact.
- **Cliff Signal:** warning tower that reveals enemy movement through passes.

### Progression branches

- **Concord Legacy:** oath councils, stronger stability, better defensive alliances in story.
- **Emergency Power:** militarized cantons, faster elite infantry, unrest from forced levies.
- **Drought Innovation:** spring engineering, lighter phalanxes, better movement through rough terrain.

### Weaknesses

- Slow army tempo.
- Poor open-field flexibility if caught unanchored.
- Limited food scaling.
- Can be bypassed if the enemy controls roads around them.

### Best counters

Lyridian ambushes can force bad facing. Veyran engineers can build alternate routes. Sareth cavalry can refuse direct fights and raid lowlands.

### Campaign role

The Orosians begin as reluctant defenders and become kingmakers because no river empire can win without iron. Their central question: “Does refusing empire simply invite a worse empire?”

### AI personality

Chooses strong ground, avoids overextension, punishes frontal assaults, retreats toward fortified passes.

## Civilization 4: Sareth Steppe Confederacy

### Identity

The Sareth are a confederacy of rider clans, caravan judges, mobile herders, and oath poets from the dry interior plains. River empires dismiss them as raiders. The Sareth see themselves as the only people honest enough to admit that fixed cities are fragile illusions.

Their tragedy is that mobility cannot preserve memory. As wells fail, they must either conquer permanent water or watch their clans scatter.

### Homeland

Open steppe, dry grasslands, salt flats, caravan wells, sparse shrubs, and long sightlines.

### Strategic fantasy

The Sareth player feels like a Mongol-style tempo genius: scouting, deception, encirclement, raid pressure, and choosing when the decisive battle happens.

### Core mechanic: Moving Court

The Sareth can project command through mobile camps instead of fixed city hubs. Mobile camps train light units, resupply cavalry, and relocate faster than normal bases.

Settlement risk: mobile camps are weaker than cities. If pinned down or forced into siege warfare, Sareth advantages fade.

### Economy

Low static production, high raiding economy, strong scouting, flexible supply. Gains resources from disrupting enemy roads, capturing herds, and controlling wells.

### Army doctrine

Mobility, feints, cavalry arcs, harassment, isolation, collapse.

### Unique units

- **Dust Riders:** light cavalry with superior scouting and raid speed.
- **Kharu Lancers:** medium cavalry that deal high charge damage against disordered units.
- **Oath Singers:** mounted command unit that improves morale during long maneuvers.
- **Well-Takers:** light infantry that capture and sabotage wells quickly.

### Unique structures

- **Moving Court:** relocatable command camp.
- **Horse Lines:** cavalry training and recovery site.
- **Signal Mounds:** temporary vision and rally network.

### Progression branches

- **Concord Legacy:** caravan law, better trade capture, less unrest in captured wells.
- **Emergency Power:** harsher raids, faster cavalry, more fear, higher rebellion risk.
- **Drought Innovation:** deep-well mapping, drought grazing, better long-range supply.

### Weaknesses

- Weak siege power.
- Poor static defense.
- Suffers against anchored spears and fortified crossings.
- Needs map knowledge and tempo to win.

### Best counters

Orosian phalanxes punish direct charges. Valeric mile forts reduce raid value. Veyran obstacles and bridges can trap cavalry paths.

### Campaign role

The Sareth are first introduced as raiders, then revealed as refugees from a collapsing interior. Their central question: “If cities hoard the river, what law binds the thirsty who never signed the city law?”

### AI personality

Constant scouting, raids exposed logistics, avoids fortress fights, attacks when armies split.

## Civilization 5: Veyran Gatewrights

### Identity

The Veyrans are engineers, mathematicians, bridge architects, gatekeepers, and siege theorists. They maintained the Seven Gates for centuries. When the drought begins, they conclude that elected councils and regional claims are too slow. Someone must control the gates scientifically, even if the world calls it tyranny.

Their tragedy is technocratic arrogance. They are often correct about systems and often wrong about people.

### Homeland

Upper-mid river choke points, ancient gates, bridge cities, foundries, dry canals, quarry towns, and hydraulic fortresses.

### Strategic fantasy

The Veyran player feels like a battlefield architect: reshape the map, create kill zones, force crossings, break fortresses, and win before the enemy understands the geometry.

### Core mechanic: Works of Necessity

Veyrans can build advanced fieldworks: temporary walls, bridge locks, siege roads, siphon towers, gate controls, and obstruction grids. Their engineers can turn neutral terrain into doctrine terrain.

Cost risk: fieldworks are expensive and slow. A failed plan wastes huge resources.

### Economy

Strong stone, iron, and engineering output. Moderate food. High build costs. Excellent siege logistics.

### Army doctrine

Terrain control, siegecraft, chokepoints, forced movement, engineered battlefields.

### Unique units

- **Gatewright Engineers:** elite engineers that build and repair advanced works.
- **Siphon Guard:** heavy infantry trained to defend waterworks and chokepoints.
- **Stone Throw Crews:** siege crews with setup time but powerful structure pressure.
- **Chain Wardens:** anti-cavalry troops that deploy temporary barriers.

### Unique structures

- **Field Lock:** controls movement across a crossing or road node.
- **Siphon Tower:** stores and redistributes water locally, vulnerable to sabotage.
- **Siege Yard:** improves siege crews and engineer build speed.

### Progression branches

- **Concord Legacy:** repair old gates, lower unrest, better infrastructure legitimacy.
- **Emergency Power:** militarized gates, faster siege, more authoritarian strain.
- **Drought Innovation:** mobile pumps, water routing, flexible fieldworks.

### Weaknesses

- Expensive units and structures.
- Vulnerable while setting up.
- Can be outmaneuvered on open maps.
- Overbuilding delays army size.

### Best counters

Sareth can raid before works finish. Lyridians can sabotage. Ithrians can adapt and counter-plan.

### Campaign role

The Veyrans are the faction everyone needs and everyone fears. Their central question: “When survival is mathematical, who gets to solve the equation?”

### AI personality

Builds chokepoints, protects engineers, uses siege patiently, tries to force the player into bad crossings.

## Civilization 6: Lyridian Reed Marches

### Identity

The Lyridians live in reedlands, gallery forests, wet ravines, and shaded tributary corridors. They are hunters, herbalists, scouts, smallholders, and memory-keepers of the river before imperial maps. They distrust monuments and prefer hidden paths.

Their tragedy is that subtle cultures are crushed by loud empires unless they become dangerous.

### Homeland

Riparian forests, reed corridors, gullies, shaded minor streams, rough trails, and hidden villages.

### Strategic fantasy

The Lyridian player feels like a forest war tactician: ambush, misdirection, stealth routes, morale pressure, sabotage, and bleeding superior armies without direct confrontation.

### Core mechanic: Hidden Ways

Lyridians reveal and use hidden paths through forests, ravines, reedbeds, and dry streambeds. They can move and reposition in ways other factions cannot, but only in terrain they have scouted or culturally mapped.

Open-field risk: on exposed plains, their advantages shrink sharply.

### Economy

Moderate production, excellent local resilience, strong scouting and herbal recovery. They do not scale as explosively as Nareth or Valeric cities but suffer less from disrupted routes.

### Army doctrine

Ambush, harassment, traps, skirmish screens, night marches, morale disruption.

### Unique units

- **Reed Stalkers:** stealth skirmishers with ambush bonuses in cover.
- **Thorn Slingers:** missile troops that slow enemy formations and punish pursuit.
- **Path-Keepers:** scouts that reveal hidden routes and detect enemy traps.
- **Green Oath Spears:** light spear units stronger in defensive terrain than open fields.

### Unique structures

- **Hidden Hamlet:** low-visibility economy node.
- **Reed Shrine:** morale and recovery site that also preserves local legitimacy.
- **Trail Snare:** non-graphic trap that disrupts movement and formation cohesion.

### Progression branches

- **Concord Legacy:** protect local autonomy, stronger captured-city integration in forest regions.
- **Emergency Power:** more traps, faster ambush troops, higher fear and retaliation risk.
- **Drought Innovation:** water-finding herbs, dry ravine movement, resilient hamlets.

### Weaknesses

- Weak in open plains.
- Lower siege capability.
- Requires scouting and terrain knowledge.
- Can struggle to finish games if enemy fortifies heavily.

### Best counters

Valeric roads and watch posts reduce stealth. Veyran fieldworks can block hidden routes. Helakar desert troops are less affected by harassment attrition.

### Campaign role

The Lyridians teach the player that maps can be lies. Their central question: “If empire calls your homeland a corridor, what must you become to remain a people?”

### AI personality

Avoids direct fights, attacks supply, retreats through cover, punishes careless pursuit.

## Civilization 7: Helakar Salt Princes

### Identity

The Helakar live in salt basins, badlands, dry canyons, mineral flats, and fortress markets. Their culture values ration law, debt oaths, hard bargaining, and desert discipline. Other factions call them cruel merchants. They call themselves the only people who never pretended scarcity was temporary.

Their tragedy is moral hardening. They survive drought better than anyone, but survival has taught them to price everything.

### Homeland

Salt flats, mineral deserts, badland passes, canyon towns, dry wells, caravan fortresses.

### Strategic fantasy

The Helakar player feels like an attrition grandmaster: ration better, march farther, punish waste, starve enemy offensives, and win through endurance.

### Core mechanic: Ration Law

Helakar armies consume less water and suffer slower drought fatigue. They can impose ration regimes on cities to survive crises.

Civil cost: harsh rationing increases unrest and reduces growth if overused.

### Economy

Strong mineral and trade-resource economy, weak food ceiling, excellent drought endurance. They can monetize scarcity but struggle to feed very large armies without conquest.

### Army doctrine

Attrition, defensive markets, harsh marches, anti-supply warfare, durable medium infantry.

### Unique units

- **Salt Wardens:** disciplined medium infantry with high endurance and low supply consumption.
- **Canyon Bows:** archers that excel from badland ridges and narrow passes.
- **Ledger Riders:** light cavalry that raid supply and carry tax/order commands.
- **Ration Judges:** command unit that reduces supply use but increases civil strain if stationed in cities too long.

### Unique structures

- **Salt Market:** generates trade wealth and ration efficiency.
- **Cistern Vault:** protects stored water from raids and drought loss.
- **Debt Court:** improves resource extraction from captured cities but raises rebellion risk.

### Progression branches

- **Concord Legacy:** fair ration law, better stability, slower extraction.
- **Emergency Power:** harsher rationing, stronger war economy, high unrest.
- **Drought Innovation:** deep cisterns, desert logistics, anti-attrition mastery.

### Weaknesses

- Weak food growth.
- Civil unrest from harsh policies.
- Less explosive battlefield power.
- Can be overwhelmed before attrition matters.

### Best counters

Nareth late economy can outproduce them if protected. Sareth mobility can raid markets. Ithrian flexible doctrine can avoid attrition traps.

### Campaign role

The Helakar look villainous at first because they talk in prices during famine. Later, the player sees they predicted the collapse earlier than anyone. Their central question: “Is mercy real if it spends tomorrow’s water today?”

### AI personality

Avoids wasteful battles, targets enemy supply, forces long marches, retreats into badlands, wins ugly but fair.

## Civilization 8: Ithrian Scholarchate

### Identity

The Ithrians are academy cities, military philosophers, archivists, legal theorists, and tactical mathematicians. They taught many other civilizations the old sciences of command. During the drought, they fracture between pacifist archivists, war theorists, and emergency planners.

Their tragedy is analysis paralysis. They understand too much and decide too late.

### Homeland

Library cities, observatories, dry lake academies, old battlefields used as training grounds, and neutral crossroads.

### Strategic fantasy

The Ithrian player feels like a high-skill adaptive strategist: read the enemy, alter doctrine, counter compositions, and win through superior decision-making rather than raw bonuses.

### Core mechanic: Doctrine Mirror

Ithrians can study enemy behavior and unlock temporary counter-doctrines during a match. They do not start with overwhelming bonuses, but they become better the longer they survive and the more they observe.

Timing risk: they are fragile if attacked before their knowledge engine matures.

### Economy

Strong research and command. Average food, water, and materials. Needs planning to convert knowledge into power.

### Army doctrine

Adaptive combined arms, commander abilities, counter-builds, battlefield analysis.

### Unique units

- **Scholarch Cadre:** command unit that accelerates doctrine learning near battles.
- **Mirror Guard:** flexible infantry that can shift stance between shield, spear, and sword emphasis with cooldowns.
- **Archive Scouts:** scouts that gather enemy doctrine data faster.
- **Proof Engines:** light siege/engineering crews that expose enemy fortification weaknesses.

### Unique structures

- **War Academy:** unlocks doctrine adaptation and commander traits.
- **Archive Court:** stores discovered enemy doctrine and improves campaign codex rewards.
- **Trial Field:** trains units with selectable veteran traits.

### Progression branches

- **Concord Legacy:** preserve knowledge, better stability, stronger captured scholar integration.
- **Emergency Power:** militarized academies, faster doctrine learning, internal schism risk.
- **Drought Innovation:** climate science, counter-drought planning, flexible logistics.

### Weaknesses

- Fragile early game.
- Requires scouting and survival.
- Less raw economy than Nareth, less raw defense than Orosia.
- Poor players may feel underpowered if they do not adapt.

### Best counters

Fast Sareth raids can kill them before they scale. Veyran hard geometry can force them into costly responses. Valeric pressure can deny scouting.

### Campaign role

The Ithrians preserve the memory of why the Concord mattered, but their theories become weapons. Their central question: “If wisdom cannot prevent war, can it at least make war shorter?”

### AI personality

Scouts heavily, changes composition, counters repeated player habits, feels intelligent rather than stat-inflated.

## Civilization relationship web

### Valeric and Nareth

Old protector versus downstream dependent. Valerics think they keep Nareth alive through road order. Nareth think Valerics use protection as a leash.

### Valeric and Sareth

Road empire versus mobile court. Valerics call Sareth lawless. Sareth call Valerics road-bound jailers.

### Nareth and Veyran

Farmers versus gate engineers. Nareth need gates open. Veyrans believe gates must be centrally controlled to prevent irrational withdrawals.

### Orosian and everyone

The highlands want neutrality, but their iron and springs make neutrality impossible.

### Lyridian and Valeric

The Valerics see forests as corridors to secure. Lyridians see roads as knives through living land.

### Helakar and Nareth

Helakar ration law versus Nareth relief law. Both are morally serious. Both think the other will doom civilization.

### Ithrian and Veyran

Theory versus engineering power. Ithrians warn that models without legitimacy fail. Veyrans warn that legitimacy without water is theater.

## Duel mode faction difficulty

- Beginner friendly: Valeric, Nareth.
- Intermediate: Orosian, Helakar, Veyran.
- Advanced: Sareth, Lyridian.
- Expert: Ithrian.

This should be communicated clearly in the UI without implying weaker or stronger.

## Faction selection UI language

Each faction card should show:

- **Survival thesis:** what this civilization believes will save the world.
- **Battle rhythm:** how it wins fights.
- **Economy rhythm:** how it scales.
- **Warning:** the mistake that loses games.
- **Countered by:** one or two enemy styles.

Example:

**Valeric Road League**

- Survival thesis: Order must move faster than famine.
- Battle rhythm: Layered lines, roads, forts, disciplined pushes.
- Economy rhythm: Reliable connected city networks.
- Warning: Roads become chains when overextended.
- Countered by: raiders, ambushers, road sabotage.

## Campaign integration

The campaign should force the player to play as multiple civilizations before choosing a final allegiance. This prevents simplistic morality and teaches mechanics naturally.

Suggested first eight campaign chapters:

1. **The Low Gauge:** play Nareth, save farms during the first water failure.
2. **Milepost Fires:** play Valeric, reopen a road under raid pressure.
3. **The Uncounted Wells:** play Sareth, capture wells before a clan migration collapses.
4. **Iron Neutrality:** play Orosian, defend a pass without declaring empire.
5. **The First Closed Gate:** play Veyran, secure a hydraulic gate before mobs break it.
6. **Map of False Roads:** play Lyridian, ambush an army crossing sacred groves.
7. **The Price of Mercy:** play Helakar, choose between ration survival and revolt risk.
8. **Proof of War:** play Ithrian, survive long enough to learn and counter two enemy doctrines.

After these chapters, the world war opens and the player can choose a campaign path.

## Balance rules for implementation

1. Every faction bonus must have a map, timing, or civil-cost vulnerability.
2. No faction gets permanent superiority in all three of economy, mobility, and siege.
3. Every unique unit must answer a specific tactical question.
4. Every faction must have at least one way to win short, one way to win medium, and one way to win long.
5. Civil strain should never feel like punishment for conquest. It should make conquest choices interesting.
6. AI should play the faction identity before it plays generic optimal strategy.
7. Duel maps must preserve fairness without erasing faction differences.

## First playable trio recommendation

For the first vertical slice, build these three:

1. **Valeric Road League:** teaches normal combined arms, roads, forts, supply.
2. **Sareth Steppe Confederacy:** teaches mobility, raids, scouting, anti-road play.
3. **Veyran Gatewrights:** teaches engineers, chokepoints, siege, map control.

This trio creates a strong tactical triangle:

- Valeric beats careless Sareth by fortifying roads.
- Sareth beats slow Veyran by raiding setup phases.
- Veyran beats predictable Valeric by controlling crossings and roads.

Add Nareth fourth to introduce reservoir economy and defensive scaling.

## Tone guidance

The writing should feel intelligent, tragic, and playable. Avoid lore dumps during battle. Use short battlefield lines, commander warnings, codex entries, and post-battle reflections.

Good line:

> “The gate was built to divide drought evenly. Today it divides guilt.”

Bad line:

> “We are evil and we want all the water.”

Good line:

> “A road is a promise. A legion is what remains when the promise is doubted.”

Bad line:

> “Our faction has plus ten infantry and better economy.”

## Final creative target

Proteus 218 should not feel like a history skin. It should feel like a new strategic mythology: Rome without Rome, Sparta without Sparta, Mongol tempo without copy-paste nomads, hydraulic crisis without modern politics, and conquest without mindless villainy.

The player should finish a match thinking:

“I did not just beat a faction. I defeated a theory of survival.”

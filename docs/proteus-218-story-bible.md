# Proteus 218 Story Bible

## Core dramatic question

When the only shared source of clean water begins to fail, can a civilization preserve justice without surrendering survival?

The answer should stay uncomfortable. The player can win, but every conquest changes who gets water, who gets displaced, and what law means after scarcity breaks the old world.

## Tone rules

- No clear villain faction.
- No gore, torture spectacle, or shock violence.
- Brutality comes from drought, impossible decisions, betrayal of treaties, exhausted armies, abandoned roads, and conquered cities under rationing.
- Each faction has rational fears and at least one legitimate grievance.
- Dialogue should sound educated, martial, and civic, closer to Roman/Hellenistic political rhetoric than modern slang.
- Avoid parody names. Every culture should feel like it could have evolved from region, resource pressure, and institutions.

## The planet

Proteus 218 is a smaller Earth-like rocky planet with a single supercontinent. It has no oceans, but it has polar icefields, underground aquifers, inland salt basins, storm-fed highlands, and one massive fresh-water river system: the Aurel.

The Aurel is not just a river. It is law, calendar, road, border, school, granary, shrine, and military objective. Most population centers are within logistical reach of it or its major tributaries.

## The Long Withering

The climate catastrophe is gradual enough for denial and sudden enough for panic. The polar meltfields recede. Highland storms shift. Tributaries become seasonal. River barges ground on exposed beds. Aqueducts draw silt. Reservoir states close gates. Downstream cities riot. Upstream cities militarize crossings.

The first war is not declared. It is improvised: ration escorts become armed columns; bridge guards become armies; treaty inspectors become spies; water engineers become sabotage targets.

## Inciting incident: the First Gate Closure

At the Ilyr canal gate of Vel Atris, water officers close a treaty sluice three days early to preserve their district's reservoirs. The decision saves local children and condemns downstream farms. Aurelian magistrates arrive with legal writs. Veyr outriders arrive to escort grain caravans through the confusion. Kharu observers warn that everyone is misreading the drought. A soldier panics, a banner falls, and the first formation advances across a bridge that has been neutral for nine centuries.

No one in the scene believes they are starting a world war.

## Major factions in story terms

### Aurelian Concord

The old center of river law. The Concord believes civilization survives through roads, courts, legions, and enforceable treaties. Its tragedy is legal arrogance: it assumes order can be restored by the same institutions that scarcity has already hollowed out.

### Veyr Steppe Clans

Mobile peoples of the dry corridors. They remember being dismissed as raiders by settled powers, even though their scouts understand drought migration better than anyone. Their tragedy is tempo: they can move faster than collapse, but not always build what comes after.

### Kharu Basin League

Reservoir states born in scarcity. Kharu culture treats water accounting as sacred civic duty. Their tragedy is cold necessity: they are often correct about rationing, but their correctness can look monstrous to those left downstream.

### Thessari Academies

Hill cities of scholars, engineers, and siege mathematicians. They warned that the compact's water models were outdated. Their tragedy is technocratic pride: they believe correct analysis should grant authority.

### Maro Reed Cities

Marsh and delta communities adapted to variable water. They see empire as a danger to local knowledge. Their tragedy is mistrust: secrecy protects them until it makes them look treacherous.

### Durnic Ironholds

Mountain fortress societies with ore, stone, and harsh winters. Their tragedy is strategic conservatism: they can endure almost anything, but endurance can become refusal to help.

### Selene Orchard Principalities

Terraced agricultural states of festivals, archery, and civic morale. Their tragedy is abundance memory: they are slow to believe the old seasons are gone.

### Oron Red Marches

Badland confederations on salt roads and hard borders. They know central powers only remember them during war. Their tragedy is rage: shock conquest feels like justice until it destroys order.

### Ilyr Canal Wardens

Gatekeepers of canals, bridges, locks, and irrigation works. Their tragedy is infrastructure dependency: every choice looks like sabotage to someone.

### Nadir Free Camps

Displaced frontier leagues formed from refugees, mercenaries, exiles, and broken civic orders. Their tragedy is identity: they can become a new model of survival or a permanent war economy.

## Campaign structure

### Act I: River Law Cracks

Teach scouting, crossings, basic city policy, and formation combat. The player sees limited engagements that leaders still call incidents, not war.

Key missions:

1. **The Neutral Bridge** - secure or contest Vel Atris without understanding the full consequences.
2. **Three Days of Silt** - keep a city supplied while the river channel drops.
3. **The Inspector's Escort** - protect treaty officials through a zone where every faction has a reason to interfere.

### Act II: Tributary Wars

Teach multi-front pressure, governor policies, supply, raids, and terrain-specific counters. The old compact becomes unenforceable.

Key missions:

1. **Reservoir Edict** - choose whether speed or stability matters more when capturing waterworks.
2. **Dust Road Ambush** - learn that cavalry tempo can beat raw strength.
3. **Marsh Without Banners** - fight an enemy that refuses clean battle lines.

### Act III: The Broken Compact

Teach siege, unrest, conquered-city obligations, veteran specialization, and doctrine trees. Every power now claims total war is defensive.

Key missions:

1. **The Court That Could Not Sit** - the legal capital falls into factional command.
2. **Stone Against Hunger** - fortress warfare under rationing.
3. **The Orchard Fires** - a food-rich region becomes the decisive target.

### Act IV: Crowns of Dust

The player must end the war through total victory. Endings reflect method, not alternate win conditions.

Key missions:

1. **The Last Crossing** - multi-front final map around the Aurel's most important ford.
2. **Capitals Without Rain** - simultaneous city pressure and army collapse.
3. **Crown of the Last River** - final conquest, followed by judgment of the player's doctrine.

## End-state judgment labels

- **Stabilizer:** conquered with strong order, preserved infrastructure, and minimized collapse.
- **Tyrant:** conquered quickly through fear, emergency levies, and harsh extraction.
- **Liberator:** conquered while integrating cities and protecting populations, but at military cost.
- **Opportunist:** won through raids, betrayal of timing windows, and resource denial.
- **Exhausted Survivor:** won after severe economic and civic damage.

These are narrative judgments, not alternate victory conditions.

## Sample dialogue style

Aurelian magistrate: “The river is not yours to close because your ledgers frighten you.”

Kharu water officer: “A ledger is a mercy. Hunger without counting becomes a riot with a flag.”

Veyr outrider: “You argue at gates because your roads taught you the world stands still.”

Ilyr engineer: “Open the sluice and this district lives six days. Close it and another district dies in four. Name the lawful answer.”
